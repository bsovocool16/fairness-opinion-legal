The deal price is the answer. The DCF is the work shown.

I pulled every discounted cash flow analysis I could find in 25 years of SEC merger filings and set them beside what experts and judges do with the same method in Delaware appraisal trials. The method is identical. Who is holding it decides the answer.

How I got here: a 320 basis point argument about beta

This started with a backtest. Our pipeline builds a banker-convention model from public data and drafts the fairness-opinion section of a proxy. On a held-out deal, Utz Brands' 2026 take-private, our first model came out at a 5.0% WACC. Citi's book, filed later, used 7.9% to 8.5%.

Nothing was wrong in the arithmetic. Every gap was a choice that a reasonable analyst could make either way: two-year raw betas instead of Bloomberg-adjusted five-year weekly betas, a 10-year Treasury instead of the 20-year, a 5.5% equity risk premium instead of 5.7% to 6.7%, and a cost of debt read from the income statement instead of the bond market. Fixing the beta method alone cut our DCF midpoint nearly in half.

So the useful question is not "what is the right WACC" but "which choices do bankers make, how consistently, and why". That is a question you can answer with data, because every fairness opinion summarised in a proxy is a public record of those choices.

The price lands inside the range, a little above the middle

Across cash deals where the proxy gives both the DCF range and the price, the price sits inside the range of the time, above it , and below it . The median position is of the way up.

A DCF midpoint is, at the median, of the deal price, and half of all midpoints are within of it. For a method whose output typically moves 10% to 20% for each point of discount rate, that is a tight orbit.

The orbit tightens in a telling place. In going-private deals under Rule 13e-3, where a controller is on the other side and the special committee's opinion will be read by plaintiffs, the price sits lowest in the range (median ) and is most often below it (). In strategic cash deals the price is above the whole DCF range a third of the time. Arm's-length buyers pay control premiums that standalone cash flows cannot reach; controllers do not, and the opinion still says "fair".

Judgment comes in half-point steps

If discount rates were estimated, their endpoints would be spread across the decimals. They are not. of all endpoints are whole numbers and another end in .5. Only carry any other decimal.

Range widths are just as stylised: two points wide or one point wide covers more than half of all ranges. The single most common perpetuity growth range is 2% to 3%, and nearly half of all growth ranges are exactly one point wide. Exit multiples follow suit: end in .0x or .5x.

This is the quantitative signature of a legal standard, not an estimate. A fairness opinion has to show a range a reasonable banker could defend. Round numbers are defensible in a way that 9.37% is not, because nobody can argue you tuned them.

Discount rates ignored falling rates, then followed rising ones

From 2007 to 2012 the 10-year yield fell by almost three points. The median banker discount rate fell by half a point. Holding bank, industry and deal type fixed, each point of Treasury yield moved discount rates by points before 2013, and by points from 2022.

Two practices did the absorbing. Board books from 2009 to 2012 often used a "normalized" risk-free rate in the 3.4% to 4.1% band while the actual 10-year averaged about 3%, and equity risk premia stayed high. Duff & Phelps, the cost-of-capital reference most books cite, recommended both at the time, which is the point: the stickiness was a shared convention, not one bank's view.

It also leans one way. A higher discount rate lowers the DCF, which makes any given offer look more generous. Sticky rates on the way down are the direction a sell-side fairness opinion is happy to err.

Who you hire moves the WACC as much as what you are

I regressed each discount-rate midpoint on the filing year, the target's two-digit industry, the deal type and, last, the bank. Year and industry explain about a fifth of the variation. Adding the bank's identity nearly doubles that.

The cleanest test is a deal with two advisors valuing the same company, on the same projections, on the same day. In such pairs the median gap between their discount-rate midpoints is basis points. One in five pairs used the identical range, which says the two teams talked. One in ten differed by more than bp, for the same company on the same day.

Every bank writes in its own dialect, and a model can hear it

I trained a plain bag-of-phrases classifier on the DCF paragraphs of the 15 most active banks, after replacing every bank name, number, year and capitalised word with a placeholder. It names the bank from the paragraph alone of the time, against for always guessing the busiest bank. Trained only on paragraphs to 2016, it still names the bank on paragraphs from 2019 on of the time. These are templates that outlive the people who wrote them.

The dialects carry substance. Goldman's paragraphs are "illustrative" almost without exception. Credit Suisse and UBS lean on exit multiples; J.P. Morgan and Evercore on perpetuity growth; Centerview, the life-sciences house, models a patent cliff with a terminal decline in 40% of its DCFs. Stifel and Piper Sandler use discount-rate ranges more than twice as wide as Morgan Stanley's.

Litigation wrote the paragraph

The DCF section of a proxy is a disclosure document, and its shape tracks the lawsuits it is written to survive. In 2001 one paragraph in twenty said an input was chosen on the bank's "professional judgment and experience". By 2026 more than half do. Disclosure suits press for why each input was chosen; that phrase is the industry's answer, and it gives a plaintiff nothing to attack.

Other changes came in on the same tide: naming the capital asset pricing model, the mid-year convention, "rounded to the nearest $0.05", and a steady move from exit multiples to perpetuity growth. After Trulia ended easy disclosure-only settlements in Delaware, the suits moved to federal court under Section 14(a), and the paragraphs kept getting more defensive rather than less.

Courts converged on one equity premium. Banks did not.

Since 2010 every equity risk premium a Delaware court adopted in an appraisal falls between 5.97% and 6.22%, the supply-side figure Duff & Phelps (now Kroll) publishes, and where the tenor is stated it is the 20-year Treasury. Bankers' board books in the same period spread from about 5% to 7.5%, and split on the risk-free tenor by house: of the books that state it, Lazard, J.P. Morgan and BofA use the 10-year; Evercore and Houlihan Lokey the 20-year.

Where the same deal reached an appraisal trial, the court's WACC was below the banker's midpoint in of the 9 cases I could match. In Jarden the court's 6.94% was below every bank's midpoint. A lower discount rate is the appraisal petitioner's whole case, so this is what you would expect if banks and courts are each doing their job.

Then the Delaware Supreme Court changed the job. After DFC Global and Dell in 2017 told the Chancery court to give weight to the deal price, no award in this set exceeds the deal price by more than 3%, and the median is of the deal price, against before.

What this means for the autoproxy model

A model that wants to draft like a banker should copy the banker's conventions, not the textbook's. Concretely: Bloomberg-adjusted five-year weekly betas with a debt-beta adjustment for high-yield issuers; the advisor's house tenor for the risk-free rate; equity premia from the advisor's era and house; discount-rate endpoints on a half-point grid, one or two points wide; perpetuity growth one point wide around 2% to 3%; and the advisor's own terminal-method habit. With those settings the Utz WACC moves from 5.0% to 7.4%, and the remaining gap is the cost of debt.

The larger lesson is the one in the first chart. A DCF does not have an answer until someone decides who it is for. The sell-side banker's DCF brackets the price; the petitioner's expert finds half again as much; the respondent's expert finds a little less; the court, since 2017, mostly finds the price. Each is applying the same formula in good faith. The inputs are where the law lives.

Method and limits

Sources: EDGAR full-text search for merger proxies (DEFM14A, DEFM14C), Schedule 14D-9s and Schedule 13E-3 / TO exhibits containing a DCF, 2001 to November 2026; documents fetched. A rule-based extractor pulls the discount-rate range, perpetuity growth, exit multiple, implied per-share range, the price it is compared to, and phrase flags from each bank's DCF paragraph; deals are deduplicated to one DCF per bank per target within 18 months, giving bank-DCFs over deals. A hand check of 30 random rows found the discount rate right in 30 of 30; exit multiples and the compared price were the weakest fields, and both were tightened after the check. Board-book fields (equity premium, risk-free tenor, beta source) come from 13E-3 and tender-offer exhibits and are noisier, so I report shares and medians only.

Appraisal decisions: 44 Delaware Chancery and Supreme Court opinions available on CourtListener, each number checked against the opinion text. Several well-known decisions exist only on Westlaw and are missing. The court-versus-bank pairs are hand-matched and target-side only.

None of this is a claim that any bank or court got a deal wrong. The patterns describe conventions, and conventions are what a fairness opinion is for. Treasury yields are monthly Yahoo Finance series; this page is a draft, not legal or investment advice.

Dataset and code: research/fairness-opinion-patterns in the fairness-opinion-legal repository (5,136 bank DCFs, 869 board books, 44 appraisal decisions, and the eight scripts that build them).
